    What is Garbh Sanskar?

    Garbh Sanskar comes from Sanskrit. Garbh means womb and Sanskar means values or education, so it is often translated as education in the womb. Indian tradition and Ayurveda describe it as caring for a baby's wellbeing through the mother's food, mind and surroundings. It is also written Garbha Sanskar.

    The four pillars

    Apollo 24|7's guide to Garbh Sanskar describes four pillars.1 In this program they show up through the weekly Saturday sessions.

    AharDiet: fresh, simple, home-cooked food
    VicharThoughts: calm, positive, mindful
    AcharBehaviour: gentle movement, a steady routine
    SanskarValues: music, mantras, reading

    Myths and facts

    Myth: Garbh Sanskar guarantees a genius child.Fact: it is described as creating a foundation for a baby's own potential. It does not guarantee outcomes.1
    Myth: it is rigid and restrictive.Fact: it is described as a flexible framework that adapts to modern lifestyles.1
    Myth: it replaces prenatal care.Fact: it is described as a complementary practice. Regular check-ups stay part of your pregnancy.1

      Why women practise Garbh Sanskar

      These are ancient Indian techniques, and many women swear by them. That is tradition and personal experience, not clinical proof. Swipe the cards or tap one to see what the tradition says, what research does and does not show, and how it fits in the program.

      1 of 5
      Read all five reasons as text

      A bond before birth

      What the tradition says: Practices like Garbh Samvad, talking to your baby, are meant to create an early emotional connection before birth.

      What research says: A study of 60 fetuses at term found a heart-rate rise to the mother's voice and a fall to a stranger's voice, so they can tell the two apart.4 That shows recognition. It does not by itself show bonding.

      In the program: Garbh Sanskar, every Saturday: talking to your baby, affirmations and chanting.

      A calmer mind

      What the tradition says: The tradition holds that lowering a mother's stress gives her baby a calmer environment.

      What research says: A 2017 meta-analysis of 5 studies of music in pregnancy (1,261 women) found a modest reduction in anxiety, no significant effect on general or pregnancy-specific stress, and study quality rated moderate to weak.5 A scoping review found that most studies of preconception and prenatal stress reported null associations with cortisol.6 So we describe calm as something for you, not a proven effect on your baby.

      In the program: Daily Pranayama & Meditation on weekdays, and affirmations in the Saturday session.

      Soothing sounds

      What the tradition says: Chanting, positive affirmations and soothing sounds are traditionally said to stimulate a baby's sensory awareness.

      What research says: Babies near term respond to voices.4 Whether chanting benefits a baby's senses is part of the tradition rather than a research finding.7

      In the program: Chanting and soothing sounds in the Saturday Garbh Sanskar session. Your teachers share the chants.

      Food and gentle movement

      What the tradition says: Ayurvedic practice emphasises proper nutrition (Garbh Aahar) and gentle movement (Garbh Yoga). Apollo 24|7's guide lists a sattvic diet of fresh fruits, vegetables and whole grains under the pillar of Ahar.1

      What research says: An academic review notes there is still a lack of substantial scientific evidence to substantiate the effectiveness of Garbh Sanskar as a whole.7

      In the program: Ayurvedic ideas on food and gentle movement in the Saturday session, with Prenatal Yoga on weekdays.
